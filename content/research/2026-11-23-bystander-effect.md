# Research — The bystander effect (the smoke-filled room)

Niche: psychology / mind tricks. Format: under-60 s YouTube Short.

## The study
- Latané, B., & Darley, J. M. (1968). "Group inhibition of bystander
  intervention in emergencies." *Journal of Personality and Social
  Psychology*, 10(3), 215–221. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0026570
- Columbia University students filled out a questionnaire in a room. A
  stream of harmless white smoke started coming in through a wall vent until
  the room was hazy. The study ran for up to 6 minutes.
- Alone: 75% left to report the smoke.
- With two other "participants" (actors told to stay passive and keep
  working): only 10% (1 of 10) reported it. The rest kept working in the
  smoke; the paper describes people coughing, rubbing their eyes and
  waving the smoke away.
- Three real participants together: 38% of groups had someone report it.
- Explanation: in a group, people look at others to decide if it's an
  emergency, and responsibility feels shared ("someone else will do it").

## The tip (advice, not from this study)
- Robert Cialdini, *Influence* (1984): in an emergency, pick out one person
  and give them a clear job ("You, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance").
  This removes the uncertainty and the shared responsibility. Present it as
  advice from a psychologist, not as a result of the smoke study.

## Don't use
- The 1964 Kitty Genovese "38 witnesses" story: later reporting showed it was
  greatly exaggerated.
