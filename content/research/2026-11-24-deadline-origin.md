# Research — The origin of the word "deadline"

Topic type: History (war — Civil War) / Words
Audience: US viewers, 16–34
Date: 2026-11-24

## Checked facts (used in the script)
| Fact | Sources |
|---|---|
| In the Civil War era (from the early 1860s), a "deadline" was a line in or around a prison that a prisoner crossed at the risk of being shot | [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/your-deadline-wont-kill-you), [National Park Service — Andersonville](https://home.nps.gov/ande/learn/historyculture/deadline.htm) |
| At Andersonville (Georgia) and other prisons, the deadline was usually a low rail fence inside the walls | [National Park Service — Andersonville](https://home.nps.gov/ande/learn/historyculture/deadline.htm), [War History Online](https://www.warhistoryonline.com/war-articles/deadline-civil-war.html) |
| The word spread widely through news coverage of Andersonville in 1864 and after | [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/your-deadline-wont-kill-you) |
| Printers later used "deadline" for a boundary line on a press, and it came to mean a time limit for finishing work | [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/your-deadline-wont-kill-you), [Word Origins](https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/deadline), [Saturday Evening Post](https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2018/09/in-a-word-when-deadlines-were-dire/) |

## Points sources disagree on / left out
- The exact decade it became a newspaper time limit (often said 1920s): not confirmed; script says "later".
- Distance of the line from the wall (up to 19 ft at Andersonville): not used.
- An earlier fishing-line sense in the OED: not used.
