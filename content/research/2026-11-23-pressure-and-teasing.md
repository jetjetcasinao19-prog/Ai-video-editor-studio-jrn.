# Research — Under pressure ("I'm excited") and being teased (fogging)

Niche: psychology / mind tricks. Two under-60 s YouTube Shorts.

## 1. Under pressure: say "I'm excited"
- Brooks, A. W. (2014). "Get excited: Reappraising pre-performance anxiety as
  excitement." *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 143(3),
  1144–1158. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035325
- Alison Wood Brooks, Harvard Business School.
- Karaoke study: before singing a song on a video-game karaoke system that
  scored singing accuracy, people said one line out loud ("I am anxious",
  "I am excited", or other conditions). The "excited" group scored highest
  (about 81% accuracy vs about 53% for "anxious").
- Public speaking: people who said "I am excited" (vs. "I am calm") gave
  speeches rated more persuasive, competent and confident.
- Math test: telling people "try to get excited" improved scores vs. "try to
  remain calm".
- Explanation (safe to say): anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal
  states (racing heart, fast breathing). Shifting from anxious to excited is
  easier than trying to become calm.
- Don't say it "cures anxiety"; it helped performance in these tasks.

## 2. Being teased: "fogging"
- Source: Manuel J. Smith, *When I Say No, I Feel Guilty* (1975), a
  well-known assertiveness-training book. "Fogging" = calmly agreeing with
  any part of a criticism that is true (or possibly true), without getting
  defensive or counterattacking. It is a communication technique used in
  assertiveness training and some therapy, not a single study with numbers.
- Safe to say: "Therapists and assertiveness coaches call it fogging." Don't
  claim a success rate.
- Safety line: fogging is for teasing and criticism, not for bullying,
  threats or harassment. Tell the viewer to get help from someone they trust
  if it's bullying or keeps happening.
