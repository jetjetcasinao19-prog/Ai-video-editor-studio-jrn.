# Research — The Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972)

Niche: psychology ("dark psychology" series, government angle). Format: 25–40 s Short.

## Sources
- US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, "The U.S. Public Health
  Service Untreated Syphilis Study at Tuskegee" (timeline and facts):
  https://www.cdc.gov/tuskegee/about/index.html
- Jean Heller, Associated Press, July 25–26, 1972 (the story that exposed it).
- James H. Jones, *Bad Blood: The Tuskegee Syphilis Experiment* (1981).
- White House, President Clinton's apology, May 16, 1997.

## Facts (safe to say)
- Run by the US Public Health Service, with Tuskegee Institute, in Macon
  County, Alabama, starting in 1932. About 600 Black men: 399 with latent
  syphilis and 201 without.
- The men were not told they had syphilis; they were told they were being
  treated for "bad blood" (a local term for several illnesses). They did not
  give informed consent.
- In exchange, they got free medical exams, free meals and rides, and burial
  insurance (payments for funerals, linked to autopsies).
- By 1947 penicillin was the standard treatment for syphilis. The men were
  not offered it.
- Peter Buxtun, a PHS employee, raised concerns from 1966 and finally went to
  the press; the AP story ran in July 1972. The study ended in 1972.
- 1973: class-action lawsuit; 1974 settlement. 1997: formal presidential apology.
- Commonly cited toll: by the end of the study, 28 men had died of syphilis,
  100 of related complications, 40 wives had been infected, and 19 children
  were born with congenital syphilis. (Use only if you add the source.)
