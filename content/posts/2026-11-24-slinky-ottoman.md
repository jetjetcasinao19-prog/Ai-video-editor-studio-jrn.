# The Spring That Walked — Ottoman miniature short, ledger look, told as a voyage

- **Video:** `animate/projects/slinky/out/slinky-ottoman.mp4`
  (41.4 s, 9:16, vertical, How it Started brand). Subtitle file:
  `animate/projects/slinky/out/slinky-ottoman.srt`.
- **Hook (first line of the video):** "A falling spring became a classic American toy."
- **Summary:** In 1943, engineer Richard James was working with springs meant to steady ship instruments. As the story goes,
  one fell and kept "walking." He told his wife Betty it could be a toy. She found the word "slinky" in a dictionary
  (sleek and graceful). In November 1945 the couple put 400 on sale at Gimbels in Philadelphia, and by the usual account
  they sold out in about 90 minutes.
- **Edit notes:** 41.4 s, 9:16. A 2D animated short drawn entirely in code in the style of Ottoman miniatures (ledger look).
  The spring itself "walks" end over end from scene to scene (down stairs, off a workshop shelf, across a dictionary page,
  past a store window). No Slinky logo is drawn. On-screen: "END OVER END, STEP BY STEP", "1943", a grey warship and cranes,

- **Fact check (from WebSearch result summaries; I could not open any page in full, so open each link once before
  publishing):**
  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) 1943 and ship springs: TIME, PBS, Lemelson-MIT and the Philadelphia Encyclopedia describe James working in 1943 on
  springs to steady or measure sensitive instruments on Navy ships, when one fell and "walked."
  (2) Betty James chose the name from a dictionary (PBS American Experience, Linda Hall Library, Smithsonian magazine).
  (3) Gimbels, Philadelphia, November 1945, 400 units (TIME and the Philadelphia Encyclopedia). TIME gives the evening of
  November 27, 1945; Wikipedia says the Tuesday after Thanksgiving, which is the same day. Captions say only "November 1945".
  (4) "About 90 minutes": Betty James's recollection (Linda Hall Library) and the Philadelphia Encyclopedia. Smithsonian
  magazine says "less than two hours." Captions say "about 90 minutes."
  Items to FIX or keep out of captions:
  (a) PHILADELPHIA NAVY YARD card: the sources I found place James at the Cramp Shipbuilding Company on the Delaware River in
  Philadelphia (a private shipyard that built ships for the Navy), not the Navy Yard. I did not find a source for the Navy
  Yard. The card may be wrong. Recommend changing it to "CRAMP SHIPYARD · PHILADELPHIA" or "A PHILADELPHIA SHIPYARD" before
  export. Captions say only "a Philadelphia shipyard".
  (b) "Naval engineer" in the voice-over: Wikipedia says naval engineer, TIME says mechanical engineer at a shipyard. Captions
  say "engineer".
  (c) Spring type and purpose: sources disagree (torsion vs tension spring; stabilizing an instrument vs a horsepower meter).
  Captions say "springs for ship instruments" only, matching the video. Lemelson-MIT adds that the Navy never took an interest.
  (d) The dictionary wording: PBS says "sleek and graceful"; Smithsonian magazine quotes "sleek and sinuous in movement or
  outline." Captions use "sleek and graceful" as the video does and attribute it to the common account. The "slinky is a
  sound" legend is not used.

  (f) Holiday totals (20,000 vs 22,000 sold) conflict; not used. The "$1 each" price and "$500 loan" are in sources but
