- **Summary:** In the 1950s in Buffalo, New York, engineer Wilson Greatbatch was building a device to record heartbeats. He

- **Fact check (from WebSearch result summaries; I could not open any page in full, so open each link once before
  publishing):**
  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) Greatbatch was building a circuit to record heart sounds in Buffalo when he grabbed the wrong resistor and it gave
  intermittent pulses like a heartbeat: Heart Rhythm Society, Lemelson-MIT, WBFO and the Saturday Evening Post all describe this.
  (2) 1956 as the year, at the Chronic Disease Research Institute in Buffalo: appears in the search summaries; the video says
  only "1950s." The institute is affiliated with the University at Buffalo, but I did not confirm that wording, so captions
  say "Buffalo" only.
  (3) The wrong resistor: Heart Rhythm Society says a 1 megohm resistor was connected instead of a 10 kilohm one. WBFO gives
  the same 100-to-1 mismatch (1,000 kilohm vs 10 kilohm). The card says only "THE WRONG RESISTOR," so no number is on screen.
  (4) Surgeon William Chardack, chief of surgery at the Buffalo VA hospital, with Andrew Gage: confirmed by the search
  summaries (Buffalo VA, historyofinformation.com, UB). They met in April 1958; the first dog implant followed in May 1958.
  (5) The first human implants of the Greatbatch-Chardack pacemaker were in 1960 (ten patients that year, including two
  children). Medtronic licensed the design that year or the next.
  Items to flag or keep out of captions:
  (a) "About once a second" and the "~1.8 ms pulse": NOT confirmed in any result. The video shows "A BLIP, A PAUSE, A BLIP"; I
  could not source it, so captions say "steady, like a heartbeat" only. Check with the Lemelson-MIT page or Greatbatch's own
  account before publishing, or consider trimming the card.
  (b) Exact date and place of the first human implant: sources conflict. Most say April 1960 at the Buffalo VA hospital; the
  UB 50th-anniversary item says June 6, 1960, and one source names Millard Fillmore Hospital. Patient names also differ
  (Hennafeld / Henefelt). Captions say only "1960" and name no patient or hospital.
  (c) Earlier implant: Rune Elmqvist and surgeon Åke Senning implanted a device in Arne Larsson on October 8, 1958 at the
  Karolinska in Sweden. It failed after a few hours and was replaced; Larsson received many pacemakers over his life. This is
  why the voice-over says "one of the first." Captions do not claim "the first."
  (d) "He realizes this could keep a weak heart beating": Greatbatch's own telling of the story; the sources agree on the
  idea but not on the timeline (he met Chardack nearly two years after 1956). The video compresses this. Captions do the same.
  (e) Whether the very first model failed: the first dog implant stopped after about four hours because body fluid got past
  the sealing. Not in the video or captions.
  (f) Green pulse line, brick building in snow, oscilloscope, parts drawer, glowing resistor and the sparks are artistic. The

**Title:** The Wrong Part That Saved Hearts #shorts
**Description:**
In the 1950s in Buffalo, New York, engineer Wilson Greatbatch was building a device to record heartbeats. He installed the wrong resistor by mistake, and instead of recording, the circuit began to pulse in a steady rhythm, like a heartbeat. He realized the pulses could help a weak heart keep its rhythm. With surgeon William Chardack, he built a small pacemaker that fits inside the body, and in 1960 it was implanted in a patient. It was one of the first, and an earlier device was implanted in Sweden in 1958.
