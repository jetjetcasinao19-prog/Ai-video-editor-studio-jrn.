# Your Brain Is Keeping a To-Do List — The Zeigarnik Effect, INKBLOT short

- **Video:** `animate/projects/zeigarnik-effect/out/zeigarnik-effect-inkblot.mp4`
  (53 s, 9:16, 1080x1920, @MINDTRACEOFFICIAL-YT brand and mask logo, AI narration voice, end card
  stamped "CASE: OPEN" and "Subscribe for more origin stories")
- **Hook (first line of the video):** "Your brain is keeping a to-do list... you never wrote."
- **Summary:** Berlin, the 1920s. The story goes that psychologist Kurt Lewin sat
  in a café with his students. A waiter took a huge order with no notepad and
  remembered every item. Once the bill was paid, he could not remember a thing.
  Lewin's student, Bluma Zeigarnik, could not let it go, so she tested it. People
  did small tasks (puzzles, beads, clay) and some were stopped halfway. Later,
  they remembered the interrupted tasks almost twice as well. An unfinished task
  stays open in your mind, like a tab you can't close. That is why cliffhangers
  hook you, and why an unanswered text bugs you at night. It is called the
  Zeigarnik effect. The trick: can't start something? Just start it. Your brain
  will want to finish.
- **Edit notes:** a 2D animated short drawn entirely in code in the INKBLOT style
  (Rorschach-style living ink blots, psychology case-file look, red-pen notes).
  On-screen facts: "Berlin, 1920s", "Kurt Lewin", "Bluma Zeigarnik",
  "Tasks remembered: interrupted vs finished — almost 2x", "Published 1927".
  End card stamp "CASE: OPEN". Voice is an AI narration voice.
- **Audience:** United States (US English; times in content/schedule.md are in ET with Philippine time).
- **Fact check:**
  - The café and waiter scene is the commonly told origin story. The video says
    "the story goes". It is not documented in Zeigarnik's paper itself, so keep
    "the story goes" or "the popular story" wording in any copy; never state it as
    proven fact.
  - Zeigarnik's paper was published in 1927 in the journal Psychologische
    Forschung. It reported that interrupted tasks were recalled roughly 1.9 times
    as often as completed ones. The video says "almost twice", so copy should say
    "almost twice" and no more precise number.
  - Later studies found mixed results. If a comment or reply asks, say that
    honestly; do not invent specific numbers or citations.
  - Spelling: "Kurt Lewin", "Bluma Zeigarnik", "Zeigarnik effect".
  - The INKBLOT look is a style choice, not a claim about Rorschach or a
    psychological test. Do not add other numbers, quotes or claims, and do not
    promise the trick works for everyone.
