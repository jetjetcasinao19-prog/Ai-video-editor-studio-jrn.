- **Hook (first line of the video):** "Ever heard of a Pyrrhic victory? It's named after a king who kept winning and still lost."
- **Summary:** In 280 BC King Pyrrhus of Epirus, in Greece, sailed to Italy to fight Rome and brought war
  elephants. At Heraclea he beat the Romans, and a year later at Asculum he beat them again. But each win
  cost him thousands of his best soldiers and officers, men he could not replace. Plutarch reports that when
  someone congratulated him, Pyrrhus said that one more victory like that would ruin him. Rome kept sending
  more armies. In 275 BC Pyrrhus gave up and left Italy, and his name became a word for a win that costs too much.
- **Edit notes:** 51.5 s, fits the Shorts limit with room to spare. A 2D animated short drawn entirely in code
  in the style of Ottoman miniatures (album look), told by a meddah (a coffeehouse storyteller) to his
  listeners, with a pair of scales that keeps coming back: each victory drops a laurel in one pan, each loss a
  helmet in the other, until the cost pan hits the floor. On-screen text: "280 BC", "EPIRUS", "ITALY",
  "PYRRHUS OF EPIRUS", "ABOUT 20 ELEPHANTS", "HERACLEA · ROMAN HORSES HAD NEVER SEEN ELEPHANTS",
  "ASCULUM · 279 BC", "THOUSANDS", "EPIRUS WAS SMALL · ROME HAD MEN TO SPARE", the quote "One more victory
  like this, and we're ruined.", "AS PLUTARCH REPORTS IT", "275 BC", and a dictionary entry "Pyr·rhic
  vic·to·ry — a win that costs the winner so much it is close to defeat".
- **Audience:** United States (US English; times in content/schedule.md are in ET with Philippine time).
- **Tone note:** dramatic but true. The quote is framed as "reportedly" / "as Plutarch reports it", never as a

- **Fact check (WebSearch summaries; I could not open any page in full, so re-check the wording before
  publishing):**
  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) 280 BC Heraclea, 279 BC Asculum: Britannica (student entry), Merriam-Webster (Asculum/"Ausculum" 279 BC)
  and Wikipedia's battle pages agree. The video says Asculum came "a year later", which holds.
  (2) About 20 elephants: sources I saw give about 20 at Heraclea and 19 at Asculum. "ABOUT 20" is fair.
  (3) Roman horses panicked at Heraclea: Britannica (student) says the elephants were new to the Romans and
  decided the battle. Plutarch's Pyrrhus 17 (Perseus translation) has horses terrified by the elephants and
  bolting with their riders. Caveat: in that passage the horses that bolt first are Pyrrhus's own Thessalian
  cavalry as well as Roman ones in some retellings, and I only saw part of the text. The card says "ROMAN
  HORSES HAD NEVER SEEN ELEPHANTS", which fits the Britannica wording; check the Perseus text once.
  (4) Heavy losses among his best men and officers: Merriam-Webster says he won at Ausculum but lost all of his
  best officers and many men. Plutarch (Pyrrhus 17, Heraclea) says he lost the friends and generals he trusted
  most. I did not see the Asculum-chapter line on officers in a full text, so captions say "thousands of his
  best soldiers and officers" only as the video does and give no numbers. Casualty counts differ by source
  (about 6,000 Roman and 3,505 Epirote dead in Plutarch, via Wikipedia; Dionysius differs), so none are used.
  (5) The quote: Plutarch, Life of Pyrrhus 21.9 (some sources cite 21.8). The Perseus (Loeb) wording is roughly
  "if we win one more battle with the Romans, we shall be utterly ruined"; Dryden's older translation says one
  other such victory would utterly undo him; Merriam-Webster gives "Another such victory over the Romans and we
  are undone." The wording varies by translation, so the on-screen line is a paraphrase and is framed
  "AS PLUTARCH REPORTS IT". The Latin "Ne ego si iterum eodem modo vicero..." is from Orosius, not Plutarch.
  Some sources say the remark is only a paraphrase or a later tradition. Captions say "reportedly".
  (6) Rome's manpower ("ROME HAD MEN TO SPARE"): Plutarch and later retellings have Pyrrhus noting that Rome
  could replace its losses and he could not. I did not find a single page that I could quote word for word, so
  captions keep it general ("Rome kept sending more armies", as in the voice-over).
  (7) Leaving Italy: Britannica's biography says he suffered heavy losses against Rome at Beneventum in 275 BC.
  Britannica Kids calls Beneventum his final defeat. Livius.org says he left a token force and sailed back to
  Epirus in the winter of 275/274 BC. So "275 BC" is the date of Beneventum, and the actual departure may be
  winter 275/274 BC. Some sources call Beneventum a defeat, others a draw or costly standoff; the video does not
  name the battle and says only that he "gives up and leaves Italy". Captions do not say "defeated at Beneventum".
  (8) The word: Merriam-Webster defines it as a victory that is not worth winning because so much is lost to
  achieve it. The dictionary card on screen is a paraphrase, not a quote. Dating differs: Britannica says
  "Pyrrhic" for victories like his appeared in the late 16th century and was paired with "victory" by the early
  19th century; Merriam-Webster cites an 1836 example. No "first used in" date is in any caption.
  Items to FIX or keep out of captions:
  (a) No exact number for elephants beyond "about 20", no casualty figures, no troop counts.
  (b) Keep "Pyrrhus won at Asculum but at heavy cost" in this form. Do not write that he "lost all his officers"
  (Merriam-Webster's phrasing is strong; other sources say heavy losses).
  (c) Do not claim that Plutarch recorded the words exactly; use "reportedly".
  (d) Series: put in "Words & Symbols" (see content/playlists.md). Reason: the video starts with a phrase
  ("Pyrrhic victory"), ends on its dictionary entry, and the question is where a word comes from, like OK and
  Big Bang. "Ancient Origins" holds ancient events told as events (Marathon, Thermopylae, Hannibal); this one is
  a close second, because it is set in 280 BC, but the payoff is the word, not the war. (Marathon is in Ancient
  Origins and also gave us a word, so it is a judgment call; move it if the channel prefers era over theme.)
  (e) Sources: Britannica, "Pyrrhus" (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pyrrhus) and Plutarch, Life of
  Pyrrhus, via LacusCurtius (https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Pyrrhus*.html).
  Both URLs came from WebSearch results (the Plutarch one in a lowercase form with a "*" in the name, which is
  how that site names it); open each once to confirm it loads and says what is cited, and that chapter 21
  holds the quote. Also seen but not cited: Merriam-Webster "Pyrrhic victory"
  (https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Pyrrhic%20victory), Britannica "Pyrrhic victory"
  (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pyrrhic-victory), Britannica "Battle of Beneventum"
  (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Battle-of-Beneventum), World History Encyclopedia "Pyrrhus"
  (https://www.worldhistory.org/Pyrrhus/), Perseus Plutarch Pyrrhus (cts.perseids.org), Livius.org "Pyrrhus (3)".
