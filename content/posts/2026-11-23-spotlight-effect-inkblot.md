- **Hook (first line of the video):** "You think everyone noticed. They didn't."
- **Summary:** In 2000, Cornell psychologists had students wear an embarrassing T-shirt
  and walk late into a room full of other students. Afterward, the shirt-wearers guessed
  that about half the room had noticed the shirt. The real number was about a quarter.
  The researchers called this the spotlight effect: you feel like the main character in
  your own head, but everyone else is busy being the main character in theirs, worrying
  about their own shirt. So that awkward thing you said? Fewer people noticed than you
  think.

- **Fact check:**
  - Source: Gilovich, T., Medvec, V. H., & Savitsky, K. (2000). "The spotlight effect in
    social judgment: An egocentric bias in estimates of the salience of one's own actions
    and appearance." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 78(2), 211-222.
    Cornell University.
  - In the T-shirt study, students wore an embarrassing shirt (a Barry Manilow shirt) and
    entered a room where other students were already seated. The shirt-wearers estimated
    that about half of the observers would notice the shirt. Roughly a quarter (about 23%)
    actually did. The video says "about half" and "about a quarter", which matches.
  - The "100 eyes" grids are an illustration of those percentages, not counts from the
    study. The shirt on screen shows the name only; his face is not drawn.
  - The "awkward thing you said" line is the general takeaway, not the study's exact test.
    The paper also ran related studies (for example, people judging how much their
    contributions to a group discussion stood out), which pointed the same way: people
    tended to overestimate how much others noticed. No numbers from those are used here.
  - The effect has been replicated in related work, but sizes vary by situation and
    person. Say "tends to", never "always" or "proves".
  - The ink blots, tiny stages and "You too!" scene are illustrations, not documented
    details. The INKBLOT look is a style choice, not a claim about Rorschach or a
    psychological test.
  - Keep it explain-not-advise: this is about one effect. No mental-health or social
    anxiety advice or diagnosis in replies.
