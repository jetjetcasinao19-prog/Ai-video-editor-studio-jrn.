- **Fact check:**
  - Sources: US Senate Subcommittee on Health and Scientific Research hearings, "Biological
    Testing Involving Human Subjects by the Department of Defense" (1977); Wheat, R. P.,
    Zuckerman, A., & Rantz, L. A. (1951), "Infection due to chromobacteria," *Archives of
    Internal Medicine*, 88(4), 461–466; Leonard A. Cole, *Clouds of Secrecy* (1988);
    *Nevin v. United States* (9th Cir. 1983).
  - In late September 1950, a US Navy ship released *Serratia marcescens* and *Bacillus
    globigii* (thought harmless at the time) off San Francisco as a mock biological attack.
    The Army's report estimated that nearly everyone in the city — about 800,000 people —
    inhaled the particles.
  - Days later, Stanford University Hospital in San Francisco recorded a cluster of 11
    rare *Serratia* urinary infections; patient Edward Nevin died. The link to the test
    has never been proven; the government denied it and won the family's 1981 lawsuit on
    immunity and causation grounds.
  - The testing became public in December 1976 (*Newsday*) and was examined in the 1977
    Senate hearings.
  - "Serratia grows red" is true: *S. marcescens* forms bright red colonies.
