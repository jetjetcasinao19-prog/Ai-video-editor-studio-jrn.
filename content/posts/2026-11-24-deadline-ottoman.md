- **Hook (first line of the video):** "Your next deadline won't kill you, but the first ones could."
- **Summary:** "Deadline" first meant a line, in Civil War prison camps such as Andersonville (Camp Sumter), Georgia,
  that prisoners were not allowed to cross; guards were told to shoot anyone who did. Printers later used the word
  for a guide line on a press bed, and by about the 1920s newspapers used it for the time by which a story had to
  be done. The word then became the everyday "time limit". Whether the printers' word came directly from the prison
  line is debated by etymologists, so every caption says "borrowed" or "the word moved on" and nothing stronger.

  (3) Merriam-Webster: original sense is a line drawn within or around a prison that a prisoner passes at risk of
  being shot; the later sense is a date or time before which something must be done. First known use 1864 in the
  dictionary entry; the word-history article "Your 'Deadline' Won't Kill You" says the earliest mentions are in
  1863 diaries kept by captive soldiers. The two years differ, so no caption gives a year for the word itself.
  (4) Etymonline (French and Portuguese pages only; the English page did not appear in results, and those pages are
  machine-translated): time-limit sense dated 1920, American newspaper jargon. Straight Dope forum quoting the OED:

  influenced by an earlier printing "guideline". Saturday Evening Post: etymologists are not sure whether the Civil
  War deadline influenced the printing one. So the link is debated and the order is not settled.

**Title:** Where the Word "Deadline" Really Comes From #shorts
**Description:**
Your next deadline will not hurt you, but the first ones were serious. During the American Civil War, guards at prison camps such as Andersonville, Georgia, marked a line near the walls, often a low railing. Prisoners who crossed it could be shot. People called it the dead line. The word later moved into print shops, where it described a guide line on a press bed, and then into newspapers, where it came to mean the time something must be finished. Historians still debate how directly those later meanings are linked to the prison line. Today it simply means "get it done."
