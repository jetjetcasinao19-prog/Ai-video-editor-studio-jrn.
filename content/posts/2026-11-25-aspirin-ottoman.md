- **Hook (first line of the video):** "Aspirin started as tree bark."
- **Summary:** Willow has been used for pain and fever for a very long time, and Hippocrates is traditionally credited with
  recommending it around 400 BC. In 1828 Johann Andreas Buchner, a professor in Munich, got salicin out of willow bark. In 1897 at the
  Bayer works in Elberfeld, Germany (then best known as a dye and chemical maker), chemist Felix Hoffmann made acetylsalicylic acid, a
  form gentler on the stomach than salicylic acid, working with Arthur Eichengrün. The name Aspirin was registered in Germany in March
  1899. Who deserves the credit is still debated: Eichengrün claimed the lead role in 1949.

- **Fact check (from WebSearch result summaries; I could not open any page in full, so open each link once before publishing):**
  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) Buchner isolated salicin from willow bark in Munich in 1828 and named it from Latin salix. He was a professor of pharmacy in
  Landshut, then Munich (associate professor per Wikipedia).
  (2) Hoffmann, a chemist in the pharmaceutical lab of the dye maker Friedrich Bayer & Co in Elberfeld, prepared pure acetylsalicylic
  acid on 10 August 1897; his lab entry survives.
  (3) Aspirin was registered in the Imperial Patent Office, Berlin, on 6 March 1899 (German Patent and Trade Mark Office, DPMA).
  (4) Name: A = acetyl, SPIR = Spiraea (meadowsweet, via "Spirsäure"), IN = a common drug ending. The "-in" reason is a theory; sources
  differ.
  (5) 1949: Eichengrün claimed in a Pharmazie paper that he directed the work. Walter Sneader's BMJ reappraisal found his claim credible.
  Bayer disputes this.
  Items to flag:
  (1) HIPPOCRATES (weak evidence). Popular accounts say he recommended willow bark for pain and fever, but The Conversation notes the
  only reference in the Hippocratic writings is the Greek word "itea" (willow), the evidence is thin, and white willow bark has little
  salicin. The narration "described willow bark" overstates it. Suggested wording for captions and any re-cut card: "traditionally
  credited with recommending willow for pain and fever." The post copy below uses this softer wording. The card "HIPPOCRATES" with "AROUND
  400 BC" is acceptable as tradition, but consider changing the narration to "is traditionally said to have recommended willow bark."
  Willow's use for pain and fever as an ancient remedy is well supported.
  (2) "HIS BOSS ARTHUR EICHENGRÜN" (narration; the cards only name the two men, which is safe). Wikipedia and Sneader say Hoffmann worked
  under Eichengrün's direction. Bayer says they were colleagues of equal standing, and the DPMA notes pharmacology head Heinrich Dreser
  also had a role. The captions say "worked with" and describe the boss claim as part of the dispute. Consider changing the narration to
  "his colleague (some say his boss)."
  (3) "German professor" and "1828": fine. Note that pure crystals are sometimes credited to Henri Leroux in 1829, and Italian chemists
  Brugnatelli and Fontana made a crude extract in 1826. Copy says "got salicin out of willow bark in 1828," which is accurate.
  (4) "GENTLER ON THE STOMACH": supported (sodium salicylate in large doses commonly irritated the stomach). The copy avoids any claim
  that Aspirin is safe or free of stomach effects. Do not add one. The story that Hoffmann did it to help his rheumatic father comes from
  a 1934 footnote that the BMJ paper calls unreliable. It is not used.
  (5) Sneader date: the BMJ paper "The discovery of aspirin: a reappraisal" is dated December 2000 (BMJ 321). Wikipedia dates the
  re-examination 1999. The copy gives no year for it.
  (6) Trademark: the packet card shows "ASPIRIN" in plain text and no Bayer logo. Good. The Bayer name appears only in the narration and
  copy, as history. Bayer's own history page says Hoffmann invented it, so Bayer's view is cited in the copy as "Bayer credits Hoffmann."
  (7) Optional context, not used in the posts: Eichengrün was Jewish, was persecuted under the Nazi regime and was sent to Theresienstadt
  in 1944 (FAU alumni profile; Pharmazeutische Zeitung). The 1934 attribution to Hoffmann may have reflected anti-Jewish revisionism,
  which is a historians' view, not settled. Not mentioned in the captions, to keep the post about the origin story.
