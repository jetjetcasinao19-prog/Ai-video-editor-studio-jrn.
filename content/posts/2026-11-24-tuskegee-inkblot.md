- **Fact check:**
  - Sources: CDC, "The U.S. Public Health Service Untreated Syphilis Study at Tuskegee"
    (timeline); Final Report of the Tuskegee Syphilis Study Ad Hoc Advisory Panel (1973);
    James H. Jones, *Bad Blood* (1981; expanded 1993).
  - The US Public Health Service ran the study in Macon County, Alabama, with the
    Tuskegee Institute, from 1932 to 1972. It enrolled 600 Black men: 399 with latent
    syphilis and 201 without. "Hundreds of Black men with syphilis" is accurate.
  - The men were told they were being treated for "bad blood," a local term for several
    ailments. They were never told they had syphilis and were never offered real
    treatment.
  - Penicillin became the standard treatment for syphilis by 1947 ("by the 1940s"). It was
    withheld, and men were steered away from treatment elsewhere.
  - Incentives included free medical exams, free meals on exam days, free rides to the
    clinic, and burial insurance (in exchange for agreeing to an autopsy).
  - Peter Buxtun, a PHS employee, raised objections from 1966 and then gave the story to
    the press. The Associated Press (Jean Heller) published it on July 25, 1972, and the
    study was ended later that year. The front page on screen is a recreation of that
    headline, not a real newspaper's masthead.
  - Many men died of syphilis or its complications; dozens of wives were infected and
    children were born with congenital syphilis. A class-action suit led to a 1974
    settlement, and in 1997 President Clinton formally apologized.
  - The 1974 National Research Act created the commission behind the Belmont Report and
    US informed-consent rules for research.
  - The INKBLOT look is a style choice, not a claim about Rorschach or a psychological test.
