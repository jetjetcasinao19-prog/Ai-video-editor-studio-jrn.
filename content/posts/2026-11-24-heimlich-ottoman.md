- **Hook (first line of the video):** "In 1974, a doctor published a trick for saving choking people that had only been tested on dogs."
- **Summary:** In June 1974, surgeon Dr. Henry Heimlich described a method for helping a choking person in an informal essay,
  "Pop Goes the Café Coronary," in the magazine Emergency Medicine. He wrote that it had been tested only on dogs but that he
  believed the findings warranted public dissemination. Within weeks, a Seattle-area newspaper reported a rescue in Bellevue,
  Washington, and by August 1974 a JAMA editorial was calling it "the Heimlich maneuver."

- **Fact check (from WebSearch result summaries; I could not open any page in full, so open each link once before publishing):**
  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) Article: "Pop Goes the Café Coronary," Emergency Medicine, June 1974 (Emerg Med 1974;6(6):154-5 per one result). It was an
  informal essay (PBS NewsHour, History.com, Mental Floss).
  (2) Dogs: Heimlich wrote that it had been "tested only on dogs" but that the logic and favorable findings warranted public
  dissemination. Experiments were on four beagles, repeated more than 20 times each (search summary; one result describes the dogs
  as anesthetized).
  (3) Seattle-area save: the Seattle Post-Intelligencer reported (June 19, 1974 per Wikipedia; PBS dates a headline "News Article
  Helps Prevent a Choking Death" to June 23, 1974) that retired restaurant owner Isaac Piha used the method on Irene Bogachus in
  Bellevue, Washington. Either date is within weeks of the June article. The narration says "reported someone had already used it,"
  which is accurate. Names are NOT used in the captions (see flag 4).
  (4) Name: a JAMA editorial of August 12, 1974 first referred to "the Heimlich maneuver" (PBS NewsHour). Heimlich's formal JAMA
  paper followed on October 27, 1975 (PBS). So "THE NAME STUCK BY 1975" is correct.
  Items to flag:
  (1) "THREE beagles" on screen: sources say FOUR beagles. Fix: draw four, or change any number text to "beagles". The captions
  below say "beagles" without a number.
  (2) "Anesthetized": only one result says the dogs were anesthetized. The video does not say so. Captions say "dogs" only.
  (3) "Taught around the world": I did not find a source in my results that says this directly (the maneuver's wide use is well
  known, but the exact claim is unsupported here). Safer wording in captions: "now widely taught." Suggested fix for the audio if
  re-recorded: "now taught widely."
  (4) Rescue dispute: one result quotes Heimlich's son, Peter, saying that both early "rescues" were bogus, so the account of that
  first Seattle-area save is disputed. The narration only says a newspaper "reported" it, which stays true either way. The captions
  say "a Seattle-area newspaper reported" and never name the person. Not made a topic of the post.
  (5) "SUMMER 1974" card: the June 19 and 23 dates are just around the start of summer. Safer: "JUNE 1974." Minor; fine to keep if
  not changed.
  (6) "Pushing air up from the lungs": a plain-language simplification of Heimlich's own description. Kept as is because it names
  no steps. Do not add any more detail.
  (7) Later controversies (Heimlich's other medical claims, and later guideline discussions) exist; they are left out on purpose
  because this video is about how the maneuver began.
  (8) Sources, URLs taken exactly as they appeared in WebSearch results (not opened in full): PBS NewsHour, "Dr. Heimlich got

  Also seen, not cited: Mental Floss (https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/69482/dr-henry-heimlichs-long-battle-red-cross-over-his-namesake-maneuver),
