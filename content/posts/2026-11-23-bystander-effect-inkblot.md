- **Summary:** In 1968, Columbia psychologists filled a room with smoke through a vent.
  When people were alone, 75% went to report it. With two strangers who just kept
  working, only 10% did; they sat in the smoke, coughing and rubbing their eyes. It's
  called the bystander effect: in a crowd, everyone waits for someone else. So point at
  one person: "You, in the red shirt. Call 911." Now it's not somebody — it's them.

- **Fact check:**
  - Source: Latané, B., & Darley, J. M. (1968). "Group inhibition of bystander
    intervention in emergencies." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 10(3),
    215–221. Columbia University.
  - Smoke was pumped into the room through a wall vent while participants filled out
    questionnaires. Alone, 75% reported the smoke. With two passive confederates (who
    shrugged and kept working), only 1 of 10 (10%) reported it. Participants coughed,
    rubbed their eyes and waved smoke away but stayed.
  - The video's "75%" and "10%" match. The grid is an illustration of 75%.
  - The advice to single out one specific person ("You, in the red shirt — call 911")
    is standard first-aid and emergency guidance that follows from this research. It is
    not something tested in this exact study, so present it as advice.
  - The bystander effect is well replicated, though a study of real CCTV footage of
    public conflicts (Philpot et al., *American Psychologist*, 2020) found that someone
    intervened in most incidents; the effect is about each person's lower chance of
    acting in a group. Say "can," not "always."
  - Emergency number: 911 is the US number (audience is US).
  - The crowd, pointing hand and red shirt are illustrations. The INKBLOT look is a style
    choice, not a claim about Rorschach or a psychological test.
