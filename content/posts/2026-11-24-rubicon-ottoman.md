- **Summary:** In 49 BC Julius Caesar had spent years conquering Gaul. The Senate ordered him to give up his army,
  and bringing an army into Italy was against the law. The border was a small river, the Rubicon, near Ariminum
  (today's Rimini). In January Caesar led one legion to its banks and crossed. According to Suetonius, writing
  about 170 years later, he said "The die is cast." The crossing began a civil war against Pompey and the Senate;
  Caesar won and became dictator, and the Republic gave way to emperors. Historians still debate exactly which
  stream was the Rubicon, and the phrase now means a point of no return.

- **Fact check (WebSearch summaries; I could not open any page in full, so re-check the wording before
  publishing):**
  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) The Rubicon was a small stream between Cisalpine Gaul and Italy; crossing it with an army broke the law
  (Britannica "Rubicon" summary cites the Lex Cornelia Majestatis, which forbade a general to lead an army out
  of his assigned province). Fits "against the law" and the card "TREASON" (see item (b)).
  (2) Gallic war 58-50 BC: Britannica "Julius Caesar" says he conquered the rest of Gaul up to the Rhine between
  58 and 50 BCE. "8 years of war" is a fair round figure (58 to 50 BC, counting both ends; some count 58-51/50).
  (3) The Senate ordered Caesar to give up his command, and not Pompey: Britannica summary. The Senate's final
  decree (senatus consultum ultimum) is dated 7 January 49 BC by HistoryNet and another popular source.
  Fits "ROME · JANUARY 49 BC" on the card.
  (4) Legio XIII: HistoryNet and other popular sources say it was the only legion Caesar had with him at that
  moment (about 5,000 legionaries and 300 cavalry per HistoryNet). The video says "a single legion" and I
  kept it general in captions. The detail comes from Caesar's own account, which I did not open.
  (5) Rubicon near Ariminum: sources agree Ariminum (Rimini) was the first town taken, and its occupation
  followed the crossing, so "near Ariminum" is fine.
  (6) "Alea iacta est" per Suetonius, Divus Julius 32: the Latin is usually printed "iacta alea est", with
  Caesar first saying "let us go where the omens of the gods and the injustice of our enemies call". Some
  scholars say the manuscript reading is "iacta alea esto" (let the die be cast); the familiar word order
  "alea iacta est" is the modern form. Plutarch has it in Greek, "anerrhiphtho kybos" (let the die be cast), as a
  line from the playwright Menander. So the words vary by writer and translation; the card
  "The die is cast." is the common English rendering and the video frames it "according to Suetonius".
  (7) Suetonius's date: Britannica gives his birth as 69 CE and says he died after 122; Wikipedia gives AD 121
  for the Lives of the Caesars. 49 BC to AD 121 is about 169 years, so "ABOUT 170 YEARS LATER" on your card is
  correct. I did not find a Britannica line stating the 121 date itself.
  (8) Caesar named dictator for life (perpetuo) in 44 BCE: Britannica and Wikipedia. The video says only that
  he "wins and becomes dictator", which is also true (he held earlier dictatorships), so the card is safe.
  (9) Location: Britannica says the modern Rubicone (formerly the Fiumicino) is the official identification,
  but the Pisciatello (north) and the Uso (south) have also been suggested. Secondary sources say the Fiumicino
  was officially identified in 1933 and the town renamed Savignano sul Rubicone, with a 1991 study backing it,
  and the matter is still argued. That fits "SEVERAL STREAMS ARE STILL CANDIDATES" and the voice-over "historians
  still aren't sure". The 1933 date is from secondary sources only, so it is not in any caption.
  Items to FIX or keep out of captions:
  (a) Crossing date: sources give 10, 11 or 12 January 49 BC and the exact day is unknown. Captions say
  "January" only.
  (b) "Treason" is the video's word for the legal breach; Britannica says the move violated the law. Captions use
  "against the law" and "treason" only as the video does, never "convicted".
  (c) Night crossing by torchlight and the sandalled foot on the far bank are artistic. Suetonius has Caesar
  catching up with his men at the river and crossing in a story with a mysterious piper; he does not give a
  torchlit night scene that I could confirm. Keep "at night" and "torchlight" out of captions.
  (d) The Senate's letter "Caesar: give up your army." is a simplified dramatization of the Senate's demand.
  Captions say "the Senate ordered him to give up his army" as the voice-over does.
  (e) Do not write that Caesar crossed "alone" or "with only one legion and nothing else"; say "one legion",
  as in the voice-over, and no troop counts.
