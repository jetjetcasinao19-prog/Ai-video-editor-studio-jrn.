- **Summary:** In 1961, Yale psychologist Stanley Milgram told volunteers to give a
  stranger an electric shock for every wrong answer — 15 volts, then 30, all the way to
  450. The stranger pounded on the wall, then went silent. The man in the lab coat said,
  "Please continue." Students predicted about 1% would go all the way. 65% did. The shocks
  were fake and the stranger was an actor, but the volunteers didn't know. The trick:
  authority plus tiny steps — each shock only a little worse than the last. The antidote:
  when others refused, far fewer obeyed. So be the first to say no.

- **Fact check:**
  - Source: Stanley Milgram, Yale University. The studies began in summer 1961; first
    published as Milgram, S. (1963), "Behavioral Study of Obedience," *Journal of Abnormal
    and Social Psychology*, 67(4), 371–378, and in the book *Obedience to Authority*
    (1974).
  - The generator had 30 switches from 15 to 450 V in 15 V steps, labeled from "Slight
    Shock" to "Danger: Severe Shock," with the last two marked "XXX."
  - In the baseline study the learner (a confederate) pounded on the wall at 300 V and
    then fell silent. The experimenter used set prods such as "Please continue."
  - Before the study, 14 Yale senior psychology majors predicted that on average about
    1% (1.2%) would go to the end. In the 1963 study, 26 of 40 (65%) went to 450 V.
  - "When others refused": in the "two peers rebel" variation (reported in the 1974
    book), only 4 of 40 (10%) went to the end. The video says "far fewer obeyed" and shows
    the 10% bar.
  - Caveats: the experiments raised serious ethical concerns. Later archival research
    (e.g. Gina Perry's) found deviations from the script and that some participants
    suspected the shocks were fake. Obedience varied a lot across Milgram's many
    variations; 65% is one condition. Say "obedience can be startling," never "most people
    are evil."
  - The puppet strings, staircase and ink blots are illustrations, not documented details.
    The INKBLOT look is a style choice, not a claim about Rorschach or a psychological test.
