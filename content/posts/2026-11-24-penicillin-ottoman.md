- **Hook (first line of the video):** "Penicillin started with a messy lab bench."
- **Summary:** In September 1928 in London, Alexander Fleming came back from vacation to find a dish of bacteria he had left
  out covered in mold. Around the mold, the bacteria were dead. The mold was making something that kills germs, and he called
  it penicillin. For years it stayed in the lab. Around 1940, a team at Oxford led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain turned it
  into a real drug.

- **Fact check (from WebSearch result summaries; I could not open any page in full, so open each link once before
  publishing):**
  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) Sept 1928, St Mary's Hospital, London; Fleming returned from a holiday to find a contaminated culture plate with a clear
  zone around a mold: ACS landmark page, Nobel Prize page (1928 discovery), and several secondary sources (Lemelson-MIT, PBS
  NewsHour, National Museum of Australia, Wikipedia).
  (2) The mold was a Penicillium (identified in sources as Penicillium notatum, now classed as P. rubens); the plate held
  Staphylococcus bacteria. The video says only "bacteria" and "PENICILLIUM".
  (3) He named the substance penicillin (sources give 7 March 1929) and published in 1929: the Nobel page and Nature's 1945
  coverage mention the 1929 paper.
  (4) Florey and Chain's Oxford team (with Norman Heatley) produced pure, usable penicillin in the early 1940s; the Oxford
  sources give the first patient, police constable Albert Alexander, treated 12 February 1941 (Radcliffe Infirmary). He
  improved at first, but the supply ran out and he died in March 1941. Captions do not mention him.
  (5) The 1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was shared equally by Fleming, Chain and Florey "for the discovery of
  penicillin and its curative effect in various infectious diseases" (Nobel Foundation).
  Items to flag or keep out of captions:
  (a) Exact return date: "3 September 1928" appears in several secondary sources, but others give 9 September, and Live Science
  uses 28 September (probably the day he examined the plate). The video says only "September 1928" and "1928"; captions say
  "September 1928" and give no day. Do not add a day.
  (b) "Heatley": confirmed as part of the Oxford team and not a Nobel laureate (Oxford gave him an honorary doctorate in 1990),
  but he is not named in the video, so captions do not name him either. Add him to the pinned comment only.
  (c) The "Science Museum" source: the search returned no real Science Museum page I could cite, so it is not used.
  (d) "Saved millions": not sourced from a page I could open, so it is not claimed anywhere. Captions say only that it became
  "a real drug".
  (e) The ACS "Discovery and Development of Penicillin" page appeared in the results, but only text fragments came back (it
  covers Fleming, Oxford and US WWII production). The search could not confirm the full text. A different ACS landmark year
  (1999 for the Oxford/Fleming landmark, 2008 for Pfizer's deep-tank fermentation) shows up in results; no year is cited here.
  (f) The mosaic plate, clear ring, spreading drops, suitcase, red-brick hospital, dusty flask and row of vessels are artistic.
  The bench is an illustration, not the real laboratory.
  (g) Series: "Accidental Inventions" (an unplanned mold on a plate that became a medicine; fits like Bubble Wrap, Post-it
