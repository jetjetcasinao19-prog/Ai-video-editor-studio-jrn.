# Why the Stethoscope Exists — Ottoman miniature short, album look, told through a doctor's notebook

- **Video:** `animate/projects/stethoscope/out/stethoscope-ottoman.mp4`

- **Hook (first line of the video):** "The stethoscope was invented because a doctor felt awkward."
- **Summary:** In 1816 in Paris, Dr. René Laennec was consulted by a young woman with heart trouble. The usual way to listen
  was to press an ear straight against the chest, called immediate auscultation. Laennec was uncomfortable with that, so he
  rolled a sheet of paper into a tube and listened through it. He heard her heart better than ever, then built a wooden
  version and named it the stethoscope, from the Greek words for "chest" and "to examine." His book on the method came out in 1819.
- **Edit notes:** 36.7 s, 9:16. A 2D animated short drawn entirely in code in the style of Ottoman miniatures (album look),
  told through a doctor's notebook. The patient is always clothed and shown at a modest distance; the "ear on the chest"

- **Fact check (from WebSearch result summaries; I could not open any page in full, so open each link once before
  publishing):**
  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) 1816, Necker Hospital, Paris: Lemelson-MIT, the University of Iowa library blog and the NIH/PMC article on the
  stethoscope place the invention at Necker in 1816.
  (2) A young woman with heart symptoms: PBS NewsHour quotes Laennec's own treatise ("consulted by a young woman laboring
  under general symptoms of diseased heart"), and says it was in the fall of 1816.
  (3) Immediate auscultation (ear directly on the chest) versus his "mediate auscultation": confirmed by the Lemelson-MIT
  and PMC results. The card text "CALLED 'IMMEDIATE AUSCULTATION'" is correct.
  (4) Rolled paper tube: confirmed by several results. The Science Museum notes the device was prompted by his reluctance to
  place his ear on a young woman's chest.
  (5) Wooden version: the Science Museum Group holds an original wooden monaural stethoscope (1820s) of his design.
  (6) Name from Greek: "stethos" (chest) + "skopein" (to observe/examine). Sources say "observe," the video says
  "examine." Both are fine translations; captions use "examine."
  (7) "HIS BOOK ON IT: 1819": CONFIRMED. De l'auscultation médiate was published in 1819 (two volumes).
  Items to flag or keep out of captions:
  (a) The "patient was overweight" detail. Laennec's treatise, as quoted by PBS and Wikipedia, says percussion and hand
  application were "of little avail on account of the great degree of fatness." Other accounts stress modesty. The video
  tells the modesty version only, so the YouTube description mentions both ("by one account, also because ..."). TikTok,
  Instagram and Facebook captions leave the weight detail out.
  (b) The word "improper" in the voice-over. Sources say "awkward" and "impermissible" for a doctor placing his ear on a
  female patient's chest, but I did not find Laennec's own wording in the results. Captions say "awkward" and "uncomfortable".
  (c) Hospital: EBSCO's research starter places the episode at the Hôpital de la Charité, while the other sources say Necker.
  Captions say "Paris" only. The on-screen card says Necker, which most sources support; check before publishing if you want it exact.
  (d) The EBSCO result also gives 1819 for the obese patient, which conflicts with 1816 in every other source. Treated as an error.
  (e) The "quire of paper" wording: not found in the results. Captions say "a sheet of paper" or "rolled paper," as in the video.
  (f) The "Case notes, 1816" notebook card is an artistic device, not a real quote from Laennec. Captions do not present
  it as one. The lub-dub rhythm, lathe, blush, Seine houses and the cracked heart are artistic.
  (g) "He can hear her heart better than ever" is a paraphrase, not a quote. Captions do the same.
  (h) The wooden tube is shown as made on a lathe; sources say he turned wooden tubes himself, but I did not verify the

**Title:** Why the Stethoscope Exists #shorts
**Description:**
In 1816 in Paris, Dr. René Laennec was asked to examine a young woman with heart trouble. At the time, doctors listened by pressing an ear straight against the chest, known as immediate auscultation. Laennec found that awkward, and by one account, other ways of examining her did not help either. So he rolled a sheet of paper into a tube and listened through it. He later built a wooden version and named it the stethoscope, from Greek words for chest and examine. His book on the method followed in 1819.
