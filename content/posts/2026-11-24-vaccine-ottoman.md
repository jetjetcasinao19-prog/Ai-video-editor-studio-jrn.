- **Hook (first line of the video):** "The word vaccine comes from a cow."
- **Summary:** The word "vaccine" traces back to the Latin vacca, "cow". In May 1796 English physician Edward Jenner, who had
  heard that milkmaids who caught cowpox seemed to escape smallpox, took material from a milkmaid's cowpox sore and put it into
  a small cut on eight-year-old James Phipps. Weeks later he exposed the boy to smallpox matter, and James did not fall ill.
  Jenner published "An Inquiry into the Causes and Effects of the Variolae Vaccinae" in 1798, and the practice became known as
  vaccination.

- **Fact check (from WebSearch result summaries; I could not open any page in full, so open each link once before
  publishing):**
  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) 14 May 1796: Jenner took cowpox matter from the hand of dairymaid Sarah Nelmes and scratched it into the arm of James
  Phipps, the eight-year-old son of his gardener. Supported by PBS NewsHour, Britannica, the History of Vaccines site, EBSCO and
  the NLM smallpox exhibition. The 14 May date is the one most sources give (one Wikipedia article text says 17 May; ignored).
  (2) Sarah Nelmes and the cow Blossom: Nelmes told Jenner a cow called Blossom had recently had cowpox. The video names Sarah
  but not Blossom; the pinned comment adds Blossom. Spelling varies ("Nelmes" vs "Nelms"); the video uses "Nelmes". Jenner's own
  unpublished manuscript reportedly calls her "Lucy Nelmes" (Wikipedia's James Phipps article); not in captions.
  (3) James Phipps, age 8: consistent across results.
  (4) The challenge with smallpox matter: July 1796 per the results. FLAG: the video says "ABOUT SEVEN WEEKS LATER · JULY 1796" while the
  calendars run May, June, July. Six weeks after 14 May is about 25 June; sources I saw give July for the challenge (commonly
  1 July, which is closer to seven weeks). Captions therefore say "weeks later" and "July 1796", never "six weeks". Optional
  tidy-up: change the on-screen text to "ABOUT SEVEN WEEKS LATER" or "WEEKS LATER" if a re-render is cheap; otherwise "about"
  covers it and I would leave it. Please confirm the exact day on the Riedel or Britannica page.
  (5) 1798: "An Inquiry into the Causes and Effects of the Variolae Vaccinae" (the video uses this title). Confirmed by the
  search results on the word's origin.
  (6) Latin vacca = cow; variolae vaccinae = "pustules of the cow" (Jenner's term). Confirmed.
  (7) "Vaccination" coined by Richard Dunning: sources disagree. One account (BMJ, via search summary) says Jenner used the
  adjective around 1799 and Dunning introduced the noun in 1800 (his 1800 paper "Some Observations on Vaccination, or the
  Inoculated Cow-Pox"); the CDC-hosted Henderson article says Dunning introduced "vaccination" and "to vaccinate" in 1803; the
  OED is cited (Science Friday) as giving the French "vaccine" 1800 and "vaccination" 1803; Etymonline dates "vaccination" to
  1800. The video says "So we call it vaccination" and does not name who coined it, which is safe. The pinned comment says
  "credited to surgeon Richard Dunning around 1800 (dates vary by source)".
  (8) Variolation (inoculation) came first: Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, wife of the British ambassador to the Ottoman Empire,
  saw it in Ottoman lands (her April 1717 letter from Adrianople), had her son inoculated in Constantinople in 1717 and her
  daughter publicly in England in 1721. Sources differ slightly on the year she arrived (1716 departure, 1717 in Constantinople).
  Variolation was never risk-free. Used in the pinned comment only, worded "an older method".

**Title:** The Word "Vaccine" Comes From a Cow #shorts
**Description:**
The word "vaccine" comes from the Latin vacca, meaning cow. In May 1796, English doctor Edward Jenner heard that milkmaids who caught cowpox, a mild illness from cows, did not seem to catch deadly smallpox. He took material from a milkmaid's cowpox sore and put it into a small cut on an eight-year-old boy, James Phipps. Weeks later he exposed the boy to smallpox matter, and James did not fall ill. Jenner described his work in 1798, and the practice became known as vaccination.
