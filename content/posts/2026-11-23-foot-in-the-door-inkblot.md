- **Hook (first line of the video):** "Want a big yes? Don't ask for it. Not yet."
- **Summary:** In 1966, Stanford researchers asked homeowners to put a huge, ugly
  "DRIVE CAREFULLY" sign on their front lawn. Only 17% said yes. But another group got a
  tiny ask first: a small "BE A SAFE DRIVER" sign for the window. Easy yes. About two
  weeks later, they were asked for the giant lawn sign, and 76% said yes. The proposed
  reason: that first yes changes how you see yourself ("I'm someone who cares about
  this"), and you act like that person. So start small, then make the big ask. The
  ethical close: if someone is doing it to you, you can still say no.

- **Fact check:**
  - Source: Freedman & Fraser (1966), "Compliance without pressure: the foot-in-the-door
    technique", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Done at Stanford
    University with homeowners in Palo Alto, California.
  - The 17% is the control group (asked for the big sign only): 16.7% in the paper,
    rounded to 17% in the video.
  - The 76% is the group whose first request was the small safe-driving window sign
    (same topic, same kind of task). Other first-request groups agreed less, so do not
    say that any small first ask gives 76%.
  - The second (big) request came about two weeks after the first.
  - The "self-image" explanation (self-perception) is the authors' proposed
    explanation, not a proven mechanism. Later research supports the effect, but it is
    modest and depends on conditions. Do not write "proves" or "always works", and do
    not add other numbers or quotes.
  - Explain, never teach manipulation: the video ends by saying viewers can still say
    no. Keep that tone in all captions.
  - The INKBLOT look is a style choice, not a claim about Rorschach or a psychological test.
