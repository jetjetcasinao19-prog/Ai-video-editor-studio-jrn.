- **Fact check:**
  - Source for the story: Benjamin Franklin's *Autobiography*. The rival was a new member
    of the Pennsylvania Assembly who opposed Franklin's re-election as clerk. Franklin was
    first chosen clerk in 1736 and the episode comes the following year, so "1730s" is right.
  - Franklin asked to borrow "a certain very scarce and curious book". The rival sent it at
    once and Franklin returned it "in about a week with another note, expressing strongly my
    sense of the favour".
  - Next meeting: "When we next met in the House, he spoke to me (which he never had done
    before), and with great civility." They "became great friends, and our friendship
    continued to his death". So "friends for life" means until the rival's death.
  - The rival is not named in the Autobiography. Do NOT name him in any caption or comment.
    The tricorne hat, assembly hall, letters and wax seal are illustrations, not documented
    details. The words in the on-screen letter are a paraphrase, not a quote.
  - 1969 study: Jecker & Landy (1969), "Liking a person as a function of doing him a
    favour", *Human Relations* 22. Participants who returned their winnings as a personal
    favor to the experimenter liked him more than those asked by a secretary (on behalf of
    the department) or those not asked at all.
  - The bars in the video are an illustration, not the study's numbers. Never add numbers.
  - The usual explanation is cognitive dissonance (and self-perception): "I helped him, so I
    must like him." It is an explanation, not something the study proved. Later replications
    have found smaller and mixed effects, so say "tends to" or "can", never "always" or
    "proves".
  - Keep it explain-not-manipulate: the tip is to keep requests small and say thanks, and to
    notice why you may like people you have helped. No advice to exploit anyone.
  - The INKBLOT look is a style choice, not a claim about Rorschach or a psychological test.

**Description:**
In the 1730s, Benjamin Franklin won over a rival by asking to borrow a rare book, then returning it with a thank you note. Franklin wrote in his Autobiography that the rival spoke to him kindly for the first time, and they became friends.
That's the Ben Franklin effect: when you do someone a favor, your brain can decide you must like them. In 1969, Jecker and Landy found that people who did the researcher a personal favor liked him more. Source: Jecker & Landy (1969), "Liking a person as a function of doing him a favour", Human Relations. Later replications show smaller, mixed results, and the bars in the video are an illustration.
