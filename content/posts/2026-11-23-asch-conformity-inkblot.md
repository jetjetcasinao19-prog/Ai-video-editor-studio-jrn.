- **Summary:** In the 1950s, psychologist Solomon Asch showed people three lines: which
  one matches this one? Easy. Alone, people got it wrong less than 1% of the time. But

  stand with them.

- **Fact check:**
  - Source: Solomon Asch's conformity experiments at Swarthmore College (1951–1956):
    Asch, S. E. (1951), "Effects of group pressure upon the modification and distortion
    of judgments," and Asch, S. E. (1956), "Studies of independence and conformity: I. A
    minority of one against a unanimous majority," *Psychological Monographs*, 70(9).
  - In the control condition (judging alone) errors were under 1%. With a unanimous
    group of confederates giving the same wrong answer, about 75% of real participants
    conformed at least once (overall about a third of critical answers went along).
  - With one partner who gave the correct answer, conformity dropped sharply (in Asch's
    reports, from about 32% of critical answers to about 5–6%). The video says "dropped sharply"; the
    falling bar is an illustration, not exact numbers.
  - Caveats: the participants were US college men in the 1950s; later replications found
    conformity varies by culture and era. Many participants stayed independent on most
    trials. Say "can," not "always."
  - The name is spelled "Asch" (the voice-over says it like "Ash"; captions use "Asch").
  - The eye, mirrored row and heart are illustrations. The INKBLOT look is a style
    choice, not a claim about Rorschach or a psychological test.
