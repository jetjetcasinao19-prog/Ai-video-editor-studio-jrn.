- **Hook (first line of the video):** "Decimate didn't always mean destroy almost everything. It meant something much darker."
- **Summary:** Today "decimate" means to wipe out most of something. It comes from Latin decimare, from decem,
  "ten". In the Roman army, a unit that disgraced itself (the usual causes were cowardice or mutiny) could face
  decimatio: lots were drawn and one soldier in ten was chosen for punishment, carried out by his own comrades.
  Polybius describes it in his account of the Roman army. Roman writers present it as a rare, extreme measure.
  Plutarch tells of Crassus using it in 71 BC during the Spartacus revolt. The modern "destroy most of it"
  meaning has been in English since the 1600s, and some usage critics still prefer the original "one in ten".

- **Fact check (WebSearch summaries; I could not open any page in full, so re-check the wording before

  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) Etymology: Merriam-Webster traces "decimate" to Latin decimatus, past participle of decimare, from
  decimus "tenth", from decem "ten". The card "DECIMARE · DECEM = TEN" is correct.
  (2) Merriam-Webster's main entry gives the historical sense: to select by lot and kill every tenth member
  of a group; it also lists an older sense, to take a tenth as a tax. Its usage note says the word strayed from
  its "tenth" meaning to mean destroying or hurting something in great numbers, and treats the modern sense
  as established (in use since the 17th century). First-known-use dates differ by source (M-W: 1660;
  Dictionary.com: 1590-1600), so no year is in any caption.
  (3) Rarity: Michael J. Taylor's article "Decimatio: myth, discipline and death in the Roman Republic"
  (Antichthon, Cambridge) cites Lintott calling the practice "relatively infrequent" and Pickford arguing it
  was rare. Fits "Rome rarely used it" and the card "ONLY A HANDFUL OF CASES ARE RECORDED". I did not find a
  count of recorded cases, so the card stays "a handful" (as the video says) and no number is in captions.
  (4) Polybius 6.37-38: a tribune assembles the legion and the offenders are chosen by lot, aiming at about
  a tenth of the guilty; they are put to death by their own comrades, and the rest get barley instead of wheat
  and camp outside the rampart. Seen only as a snippet of the Greek text; the exact Polybius page was not
  found in a form I could cite by URL. (Polybius's method is the fustuarium, a beating; it is not described
  in any caption.)
  (5) Crassus, 71 BC: Plutarch (Crassus 10) says Crassus revived the old punishment for troops who had fled,
  and the revolt is dated 73-71 BC. The card says "as Plutarch tells it", which is accurate framing.

"Decimate" now means to wipe out most of something. It started as something very different. The word comes from Latin decimare, from decem, "ten."
