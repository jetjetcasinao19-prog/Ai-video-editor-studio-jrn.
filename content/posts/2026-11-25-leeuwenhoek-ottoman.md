- **Hook (first line of the video):** "The first person to see bacteria wasn't a scientist."
- **Summary:** Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was a cloth merchant (draper) in Delft with no university training. He used lenses to check thread, learned to make his own tiny lenses and built simple single-lens microscopes. In water he saw tiny moving creatures, which Henry Oldenburg of the Royal Society translated as "animalcules" (his own Dutch word was "diertgens" or "dierken", little animals). A letter dated 17 September 1683 describes what he found in plaque from his own teeth. He wrote to London's Royal Society from 1673 until his death in 1723, about 50 years.

- **Fact check (from WebSearch result summaries; I could not open any page in full, so open each link once before publishing):**
  Confirmed or well supported:
  (1) Draper/cloth merchant in Delft, trained in the cloth trade in Amsterdam from about age 16; no university education, knew only Dutch.
  (2) He wanted to see thread quality better than the lenses of the time allowed (UCMP Berkeley), which supports "he checked thread with a lens".
  (3) He learned to grind lenses by about 1668 and made simple single-lens microscopes: one small bead-like lens held between two riveted metal plates.
  (4) The letter of 9 October 1676, "Concerning Little Animals by Him Observed in Rain- Well- Sea- and Snow- Water...", is cited as the first published mention of bacteria (pepper-water animals smaller than a millionth of a grain of sand).
  (5) "Animalcules": Oldenburg translated his Dutch "dierken/diertgens/diertjes" (little animals). The card "ANIMALCULES = LITTLE ANIMALS" is correct.
  (6) 17 September 1683 letter to Francis Aston, Royal Society archive EL/L1/69, describes plaque from his own teeth and from an old man who said he never cleaned his teeth; first published drawings of bacteria forms (round, rod, spiral).
  (7) First letter to the Royal Society 1673; he wrote until his death in 1723 (about 50 years).
  Items to flag:
  (1) CLAIM "FIRST PERSON TO SEE BACTERIA". Widely repeated, but a sources caveat: Britannica says he likely saw protozoa in 1674 and bacteria several years later, and the 1676 letter and the 1683 drawings are the first published records. Safe wording: "first to see and describe bacteria, as far as the record shows". The copy below uses that softer wording and does not say "first person ever".
  (2) "WASN'T A SCIENTIST". Fair as "no formal training", but he is also called a pioneer of microbiology and corresponded as a natural philosopher. The copy says "a cloth merchant with no university training".
  (3) LENSES "GROUND". Mostly right, but not the whole story: Wikipedia notes he also made ground lenses for lower magnification, and a 2021 neutron tomography study found a glass stem characteristic of a drawn or melted bead in a high-power instrument, pointing to a bead method for his strongest lenses. My search did not turn up a source that uses the phrase "blown glass beads" or lays out the debate. The narration says "grinds his own tiny glass lenses", so the copy says "made his own tiny lenses" (accurate either way).
  (4) RESOLVED: the size card now reads "ABOUT 2 INCHES LONG". Not confirmed as one figure. A Boerhaave brass original has plates of 16 x 40 mm (about 1.6 in); other brass examples are about 24-28 x 37-47 mm; a Science Museum model is about 6 cm. So "roughly 2 inches" is a fair summary, but "tall" is odd for an instrument whose long side is about 2 inches and which was held up to the eye. The copy says "about 2 inches long, roughly thumb-sized". Consider changing the card to "ABOUT 2 INCHES LONG" if easy.
  (5) LETTER COUNT. Sources conflict: Wikipedia says about 190 letters to the Royal Society by 1723; another source says over 100 were published in the Philosophical Transactions. The copy gives no number, only "about 50 years". Do not add a count unless it is checked in the Royal Society or Collected Letters edition.
  (6) 1683 DATE. Some sources print 12 September 1683 (an error in the Philosophical Transactions and Dutch editions, per the Dutch letters edition); the Royal Society archive gives 17 September 1683. The video only says "1683". Copy says "in 1683" and, in the pinned comment, "a letter dated 17 September 1683".
  (7) "HE SCRAPES THE PLAQUE FROM HIS OWN TEETH". Supported: the letter covers his own teeth and an older man's. The copy says "plaque from his own teeth" (and the pinned comment adds the old man).
  (8) PRONUNCIATION. The voice says "Anthony" while the subtitles and cards say "Antonie". His name is also spelled Antony, Antoni and Anthony in English sources, so this is not wrong, but the subtitles will not match the audio on that one word. Optional: change the subtitle to "Anthony" or leave it. The copy uses "Antonie van Leeuwenhoek".
  (9) RESOLVED: the closing letter page now shows only an address ("To the Royal Society, London — from Delft"), no invented quotation. "Honoured Sirs, / In water I saw / little animals…" is an invented illustrative note. It reads like a quotation, because it is shown as a letter. It is not a quote. The pinned comment says so. Nothing in the captions presents it as his words. Recommended: if the card is re-rendered, add a small "ILLUSTRATIVE" line or keep the disclosure in the pinned comment.
