- **Summary:** In 2010, Carnegie Mellon researchers had people imagine eating M&M's, one by
  one. Then they got a real bowl. People who pictured eating 30 ate fewer real ones;
  picturing just three didn't help. The trick: your brain gets tired of a food just by
  imagining it, bite after bite. So next craving, picture every bite, slowly.

- **Fact check:**
  - Source: Morewedge, C. K., Huh, Y. E., & Vosgerau, J. (2010). "Thought for food:
    Imagined consumption reduces actual consumption." *Science*, 330(6010), 1530–1533.
    Carnegie Mellon University.
  - People imagined eating 30 or 3 M&M's (or did a matched control task, like imagining
    inserting coins into a laundry machine), then ate freely from a bowl. Those who
    imagined eating 30 ate fewer real candies; imagining 3 did not reduce eating. The
    explanation is habituation: repeatedly imagining eating a food makes it less
    rewarding, as actually eating it does.
  - It only worked for the same food (imagining eating cheese didn't reduce eating
    M&M's), and you have to imagine eating it, not just picture the food.
  - Later replication attempts have been mixed, so say "can help," not "will stop
    cravings." Not medical or diet advice.
  - The bars on screen are an illustration, not the study's numbers. M&M's is named only
    as the study's candy; no logo is shown.
  - The INKBLOT look is a style choice, not a claim about Rorschach or a psychological test.
